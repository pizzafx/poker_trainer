The idea

What is this?

A practice table for the games most people actually play: live $1/$2, $2/$3 and $2/$5 no-limit hold'em at a casino, or a home game with your own stakes and your own cast of players. At the casino table you sit with seven opponents who play like the people in a card room: the retiree who only raises aces, the guy who calls every flop, the kid in the hoodie, the loud one on his third beer. You make every decision yourself, and after each one a coach tells you whether it made or lost money against these particular players.

It is for players who know the rules and the basics and want to win more at low-stakes live games. It is not a solver and does not try to be.

Why not just study GTO?

Game-theory-optimal play is the strategy that cannot be beaten by a perfect opponent. It is the right thing to study if your opponents are good. At $2/$3 they mostly are not, and the way they are bad is predictable:

  • They limp and call far too many hands before the flop.
  • They call too much after the flop with any pair or any draw.
  • They rarely bluff the river and almost never raise it without a big hand.
  • They bet big when they are strong and small when they are not.

Against players like that, the solver's play is often not the money play. A solver calls a river raise with top pair top kicker some of the time so it cannot be bluffed. A retiree who has check-called twice and now raises the river is not bluffing, so that call just burns money. A solver bluffs rivers at a balanced frequency. A calling station does not fold, so the bluff burns money too.

The trainer flags the play that loses money against the people at the table, even when a solver would approve of it. And it gives a green tick to plays a solver hates when they print.

How decisions are graded

How does it decide a play is a mistake?

It does not look anything up. Every time you act it runs an experiment:

  1. Who are they? For each opponent still in the hand it uses the current read: what an attentive player could conclude from their look, their betting and their showdowns so far.
  2. What can they hold? It works out which hands fit the way that kind of player has played this hand. A nit who limped, called a raise and check-raised the turn has a very different range from a maniac who did the same.
  3. Play it out. It deals the opponents hands from those ranges and plays the rest of the hand to the end for each of your options: fold, call, and several bet or raise sizes. It does this up to 3,000 times before and on the flop, up to 1,500 times on the turn and up to about 800 times on the river (fewer in big multiway pots, or when time runs short), using the same cards for every option so the comparison is fair.
  4. Compare. The average result of each option is its value. If the option you chose is clearly worse than the best one, it is flagged.

Your own later decisions in those play-outs are made by a solid, exploit-aware stand-in. So "call" is valued as "call now and then play the rest of the hand sensibly".

What do green, yellow and red mean?

MeaningWhen
GreenFineYour play was the best option, or too close to the best to tell apart.
YellowMistakeIt gives up at least 3 big blinds and at least 10% of the pot compared with the best option.
RedBlunderIt gives up at least 8 big blinds and at least 20% of the pot.

On top of that, the gap has to be well clear of the simulation's margin of error, and it has to hold up when the opponents are made to call a little looser and a little tighter and their ranges are read a little stronger and weaker (see below). If your play is only worse under some of those versions, it is not graded. Choosing a different bet size needs a bigger gap before it is flagged and is never red; with one pair you are compared with bets up to three-quarters of the pot, and with several players in, a bet of 45% of the pot or more is never called too small. Folding before the flop is only flagged with a hand in the Tight range for your seat, and never worse than a mistake.

Grading runs in the background, so the table never freezes while the coach is thinking.

The table keeps dealing while you are green. A yellow or red play stops it so you can read why.

What does "costs about $40" mean?

It is the difference in average result between what you did and the best option, over thousands of play-outs. It is not what you won or lost on this hand. If calling loses $40 on average and folding loses nothing, the call "costs about $40" whether or not you happened to hit your card this time.

"Mistakes cost" on the session placard, in the corner of the table, adds those up for the session. It is your decisions with the luck taken out. Read it as a direction (is it shrinking from session to session?) more than as exact dollars, because the opponents are easier to beat than real people.

I won the pot. Why was it a blunder?

Because the grade is about the decision, not the outcome. Calling a nit's river raise with one pair is a losing play even on the night he turns over a missed draw. The reverse is true as well: you will see a green tick on hands where you got stacked, because getting the money in was right and the cards fell badly. This is the hardest habit to build at a real table, and it is the main thing the trainer is for.

How does it know what they have?

It does not peek. It never uses the opponent's actual cards. It starts with every hand they could hold and, at each action they take, asks "how likely is this kind of player to do that with each hand?" Hands that fit stay; hands that do not fit fade out. By the river a tight player who has raised twice has a narrow range of strong hands, and a station who has only called has a wide range of pairs and busted draws.

When a play is flagged, the coach shows you that range in words: roughly what share is two pair or better, one pair, draws and nothing, and how much of it your hand beats compared with the price you were being offered.

Can I see the range it is using?

Yes. The Ranges tab in the side panel draws an opponent's likely hands on the standard 13 by 13 chart: pairs on the diagonal, suited hands above, offsuit below. Brighter means more likely. It updates with every action, and the street buttons let you step back and watch the range narrow from preflop to the river.

The number at the top is the one to learn from: how much of that range your hand beats right now, and how that has moved street by street. Seeing "you are behind almost all of it" against a nit who just raised, or "you are ahead of most of it" against a maniac who bets everything, is the habit this trainer is trying to build. You can colour the chart by what each hand has become on this board, or by whether you are ahead of it.

It is the coach's estimate, built from how the player has acted and what kind of player they seem to be. It is not their actual cards. Early in a session, before there is a read, it blends what every type of player would hold, so it starts wide. If you would rather test yourself first, turn off "Show ranges during the hand" in Your game and ranges.

How do reads work?

Every opponent has a hidden style. What you see is what you would see live:

  • A look. Ball cap and coffee, hoodie and headphones, team jersey with one eye on the TV. Looks are linked to styles only loosely. The old man is usually tight; sometimes he is the biggest gambler at the table.
  • Their betting. Every limp, raise, call and fold shifts the read.
  • Showdowns. A shown hand tells you the most: what they called three streets with, what they raised with.

The line under each seat is the coach's current read: "Station?" is a first guess, "Likely station" is firmer, plain "Station" is as sure as it gets, and a dash means no read yet. Hover over a player for what you have seen them do. You are graded against that read, not against the hidden truth, so early in a session the coach judges your play against the mix of players a stranger could be. Click a seat to see what you have observed, the hands they have shown, and a spoiler button for their real style.

Reads never reach certainty. Even after a long session the coach tops out below 90%.

What is "why theory disagrees"?

When a play is flagged, the gold paragraph explains why a theory-trained player might have made it and why it fails here. For example: a solver calls with a hand that strong so it cannot be bluffed, and that protection is worth nothing against someone who does not bluff in that spot. It is shown for the kinds of spot where theory usually takes the other side.

What does ≈ mean in "Show the numbers"?

The values are estimates from a simulation, not exact answers. An option marked ≈ is within the margin of error of the best one: on another run it could come out on top. Treat those as equal. Clear-cut decisions almost always come out the same way; close ones can swap places, which is why close ones are never flagged.

What is "the coach's estimate"?

Under most flags and notes is one line that says what your play needed and what it got. For a bet: "this bet needs everyone to fold about 45% of the time; they fold about 15%". For a call or a value bet: "when called you are ahead about 80% and need 45%". It is the quickest way to see why a play won or lost, and the habit to take to a real table: what does this need, and is it getting it?

Why is checking into a crowd only a note?

Pots with several players have their own rules. Checking one pair or a draw into two or three opponents is usually reasonable at these stakes, so it gets a note rather than a flag; it can still be yellow when it gives up a lot, and a turn check with two pair or better that closes the action can be red. Some plays score well only against these particular opponents, such as raising a draw into a bettor and a caller. Those stay green with a "not a habit to build" note, because real players punish them.

Why does it so often want a bigger bet?

Because the most reliable way to win at these stakes is to charge people who call too much, and most players bet too small with their good hands. You are only ever compared with bets up to the size of the pot (up to three-quarters of it with one pair), and in a pot with several players a bet of 45% of the pot or more is never marked as too small.

How many hands before a read means anything?

About 30 hands separates the extremes: you will know the nit from the gambler. Telling neighbours apart, such as a solid regular from a tricky one, takes much longer. If you want to skip the wait, Quick sim (under More (⋯) in the header, or on the placard before your first hand) plays 40 hands in an instant so you start with something observed about everyone; your results in those hands do not count.

Can I go back over a hand?

Yes. Your last 100 hands are kept in your browser. Hand history in the header lists them, and you can filter to flagged hands. Open one, or press "Replay" in any hand review, and step through it action by action: the pot and the price at each step, the coach's verdict at each of your decisions, the opponents' ranges as they stood at that moment, and "Show all cards" to see what everyone held. The Hand log in the side panel shows the current hand street by street, and earlier hands from tonight open in the replayer.

The opponents

Who am I playing against?

TypeHow they play
Tight-passive nitWaits for big hands, limps a lot, rarely raises. When the money goes in, he has it.
Straightforward recreationalBets when he hits, gives up when he misses, limps along.
Calling stationSees lots of flops and hates folding a pair or a draw. Raises only with a big hand.
GamblerPlays most hands, calls far too much, fires the odd wild bluff.
ManiacRaises and barrels constantly. His big bets mean much less.
Solver-style playerSmall bets, balanced bluffs, by the chart whoever he is up against.
Solid regularTight-aggressive grinder who isolates limpers and respects passive players' raises.
Tricky regularPlays many hands in position and applies pressure, with better timing than the maniac.
Total beginnerHome games only. Plays most hands, bets small, and gets scared off by big bets.

No two players of a type are identical: each is a little looser or tighter, more or less sticky than the template. They buy in for different amounts, some sit short, they leave and get replaced, and they go on tilt after losing a big pot (sometimes visibly). The table also notices you. Raise and bluff a lot and you start getting called lighter for a while; get caught bluffing at showdown and it sticks.

Where do their tendencies come from?

From research into how these games are played, and an honest gap in that research.

  • Before the flop there is real data. The one tracked sample of low-stakes live play found about 35% of hands played, 6% raised, four players seeing the average flop, and limpers calling a raise a little over half the time. The opponents here match it closely: about 35% of hands played, 7% raised, and three and a half players per flop.
  • After the flop there is none. Nobody has published how often live low-stakes players fold to a bet of each size, or how often their river raises are bluffs. What exists is strong agreement among coaches about the direction: they call too much, bluff rivers too little, and their raises are real. The opponents follow that direction; the exact numbers are expert judgement. Some have been checked against a three-player solver on the river and turn, none against live hand data.

The full report, with every source and a list of what could not be found, is in the project's reports folder.

Trust

How much should I trust it?

Trust most: fold one pair to a passive player's raise. Value-bet more often and bigger against players who call. Stop bluffing people who do not fold. Raise big over limpers. Stop calling raises with hands that are dominated. These rest on the strongest evidence and on what coaches agree about.
Trust the direction, not the dollars: the size of each "costs about $X". The opponents are easier to beat than real people, so the figures run high.
Trust least: any advice to bluff, and any advice in very deep-stacked or very large pots. These depend most on the assumed numbers.

One number to keep in mind: a player who blindly took the coach's top-rated option on every decision would win around 200 big blinds per 100 hands here, many times the 15 to 30 a strong player makes in a real $1/$2 or $2/$5 game. A steady, disciplined player wins about 25 to 30. That gap is the measure of how much easier these opponents are than the real thing. Trust the direction of the advice more than the dollar figures, and use the trainer to build habits, not to estimate your hourly rate.

Is it saying I should never bluff-raise?

No. Bluff-raising is sometimes right at these stakes: against a regular who bets the flop with everything and gives up when raised, or with a big draw that can also get there. It is just right far less often than in theory, because most of the pool does not bet without a hand and does not fold one once they have it.

What the trainer does is one-sided on purpose:

  • If you bluff-raise, it is judged like any other play. Against the right opponent it gets a green tick. Into a calling station it gets flagged, with the cost.
  • If you do not bluff-raise, you are never marked down for it, even when the simulation rated the raise higher. The coach will tell you it rated higher and leave your play green.

The reason for the lopsidedness: how often live players fold to a raise is the least reliable number in the whole model. Nobody has measured it, and simulated opponents fold a little too predictably, so a simulation left alone finds bluff-raises everywhere. Telling you "you should have raised with nothing" on the strength of that number would build an expensive habit on a guess. Not bluff-raising in a spot where it was right costs you a little now and then; bluff-raising people who do not fold costs you stacks.

The same caution is applied in three other places:

  • Overbets. Whatever you hold, you are compared with the best bet up to the size of the pot, so you are never marked down for not shoving.
  • Bluffs you pass up. Checking a weak hand is never flagged against players read as unable to fold (stations, gamblers, maniacs). Against players who can fold it is flagged only when betting was better by a wide margin, wider still on the river, and never in red.
  • Re-raise bluffs before the flop. You are not marked down for declining to 3-bet a hand that is not a real value hand.
  • Other versions of the opponents. Every flag is re-checked with the opponents calling a little looser and a little tighter, and with their ranges read a little stronger and weaker. If your play is only worse under some of those versions, it is not graded.
  • Checks before the river. A check before the river is never red, except a turn check with two pair or better that closes the action.

So read a green tick with a note like "a raise scored higher against these bots" as: fine play, and here is something to think about, not something to copy at the casino tomorrow.

My game plays differently. Can I change it?

Yes. The Players button opens sliders for each type: how many hands they play and raise, how often they bluff, the weakest hand they call a river bet with, the hand they never fold, how often they chase draws, and how common the type is. If the stations in your room fold more than these do, move the slider. Changes are saved in your browser.

In Your game and ranges you can also pick the table: typical, aggressive, conservative, loose and passive, or tough (more regulars, fewer calling stations and gamblers, the hardest to beat). It is the same cast in a different mix.

Modes and settings

What is drill mode?

Every flagged play is filed under a leak: paying off, folding too much, bluffing callers, missed value, passing up a profitable bet, overplaying, sizing, chasing draws, strong hands played wrong, and four preflop leaks. Leak review ranks them by what they have cost you across all your sessions. Press Drill on one and the trainer deals only that kind of spot, playing the earlier streets for you and stopping at the decision. One decision, one verdict, next spot. If your leak has only shown up on the turn, it deals turn spots.

What is home game mode?

Your own stakes and your own cast. Set the blinds and buy-in, then choose how many of each type sit down: three maniacs, two nits and a total beginner, say. There is no rake, everyone rebuys, and nobody leaves. You know who is coming, but you still have to work out which seat is which.

Why were hands folded for me?

Hands outside your ranges are folded for you, so your time goes on decisions that matter. Those hands still count: you pay your blinds, the button moves, and you keep learning about the table from the hands you sit out. The Hand log shows them as "Hands 12–15 folded for you".

Your ranges are in Your game and ranges → My ranges. There is one per seat for when you are first in and one for facing a raise. Start from Tight, Standard or Loose, or paint your own on the hand chart; each seat shows how much looser than Standard you are, with an amber dot on every hand Standard folds. You get there from any "Edit my ranges" link too, in the Hand log or under a tip. Turn off "Fold hands outside my ranges" to play every hand.

What happens if I reload or close the tab?

The session carries on. The table picks up at the next hand with the same players, stacks and reads. Only one browser tab runs the table at a time; open it in a second tab and that tab offers to take over.

Does it cover tournaments, online play or other games?

No. It is built for live, full-ring, low-stakes cash games: deep-ish stacks, raked pots, lots of limping, opponents you can see. Tournament stack sizes and payout pressure change the right play, and online pools are tighter and more aggressive. The default rake is a typical 10% capped rake; buy-ins follow common card-room ranges. Other rake structures are in Your game and ranges.

Where does my data go?

Nowhere. Everything runs on your own computer in your browser. Your settings, your ranges, your tracked leaks, your last 100 hands, the session in progress and any changes you make to the players are stored in that browser only. Clearing the browser's site data resets them.
